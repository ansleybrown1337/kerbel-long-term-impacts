PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY 
The adoption of conservation tillage on farmlands continues to increase, providing economic and 
environmental benefits, but its long-term effects on runoff water quality remain poorly 
understood. Previous work has focused on short studies or small plot areas, making it unclear 
whether tillage effects extend beyond a single season. This project analyzed runoff water quality 
from a production-scale, surface-irrigated conservation tillage study field monitored from 2011 
to 2025. Because long-term data often contain gaps, changing practices, and evolving 
measurement methods, a Bayesian statistical approach was used to account for these challenges 
rather than overlook them. Results show that more intensive tillage incurred more sediment and 
sediment-bound nutrient loss, including phosphorus and organic nitrogen. Some tillage-analyte 
effects persisted across years. These findings provide evidence that conservation tillage can 
protect runoff water quality beyond the year it is applied. 
ABSTRACT 
Understanding how tillage practices influence runoff water quality is critical for evaluating 
agricultural management, but few studies have examined long-term effects in operational 
surface-irrigated systems. We analyzed a 2011 to 2025 edge-of-field monitoring record from 
conventional tillage (CT), minimum tillage (MT), and strip tillage (ST) systems in Fort Collins, 
Colorado. Tillage disturbance was represented with the NRCS Soil Tillage Intensity Rating 
(STIR), allowing disturbance intensity to be compared across years with changing crop rotations 
and field operations. A hierarchical Bayesian causal framework estimated direct STIR effects on

Page 3 of 73 
 
residue cover, concentration, and volume which propagated uncertainty from measurement error, 
censoring, missing observations, and evolving field methods; and characterized temporal 
structure with a multi-output Gaussian process. Greater STIR was most associated with increased 
concentrations and loads of particulate-associated constituents, especially total suspended solids 
(TSS), total phosphorus (TP), and total Kjeldahl nitrogen (TKN-N), while dissolved constituent 
responses were weaker or more variable. Across 2011 to 2025, MT and ST reduced cumulative 
export of TSS by 29.4% and 24.2%, TKN-N by 31.3% and 26.9%, TP by 15.9% and 16.6%, and 
total nitrogen by 19.6% and 17.8%, respectively, relative to CT. Temporal covariance suggested 
some nutrient patterns, particularly TN-N and TP, persisted up to 3 years, whereas TSS 
responses were more immediate and closely linked to current residue and runoff. These results 
support reduced tillage as a practical conservation strategy for limiting sediment and sediment-
associated nutrient losses, while highlighting the need for complementary strategies to address 
dissolved constituents. 
1 INTRODUCTION 
Water quality is a critical component of environmental sustainability, ecosystem function, and 
human health, with widespread implications for drinking water supplies, aquatic habitat integrity, 
and downstream water use (Carpenter et al., 1998; V. H. Smith et al., 1999). Globally, 
agriculture is one of the dominant contributors to nonpoint source pollution, exporting sediments, 
nutrients, and agrochemicals from managed landscapes into surface waters through runoff 
processes (Foley et al., 2011; Kleinman & Harmel, 2024; Tilman et al., 2002). These losses are 
driven by the interaction of climatic forcing, soil properties, and management practices, and can 
lead to eutrophication, hypoxia, and degradation of freshwater and coastal systems (Diaz & 
Rosenberg, 2008; Hopkins et al., 2025; Kleinman et al., 2011, 2015). As a result, improving 

--- PAGE 4 ---
 
Page 4 of 73 
 
water-quality outcomes in agricultural systems has become a central focus of both research and 
policy, requiring a better understanding of how management practices influence hydrologic 
pathways and constituent transport across spatial and temporal scales (Harmel, Pampell, et al., 
2018; D. R. Smith et al., 2018; Trotter et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). 
Among agricultural management practices, tillage plays a particularly important role in shaping 
water quality outcomes because it directly alters soil physical properties that govern hydrologic 
response and erosion processes at the field scale by modifying soil structure, residue cover, and 
aggregate stability. Tillage influences infiltration, surface runoff generation, and the mobilization 
and transport of both sediment-bound and dissolved constituents. Multiple short-term and plot-
scale studies, particularly in rainfed systems, have documented these relationships (Montgomery, 
2007; Prasad, Thompson, et al., 2023). In addition, several long-term watershed and field-scale 
investigations in rainfall-driven environments have demonstrated that conservation tillage and 
nutrient management substantially influence runoff, sediment, and nutrient export over long-term 
time scales (Carver et al., 2022; Harmel et al., 2014; Nelson et al., 2023; Nonxuba et al., 2026; 
Sun et al., 2026). However, relatively fewer investigations have characterized these effects in 
surface-irrigated fields under operational conditions. This distinction is important because furrow 
irrigation introduces managed inflow, tailwater runoff, and irrigation-induced erosion processes 
that differ fundamentally from rainfall-driven runoff (Bjorneberg, Westermann, et al., 2006; 
Sojka, Bjorneberg, & Strelkoff, 2007; Sojka, Bjorneberg, Entry, et al., 2007). Research in semi-
arid furrow-irrigated systems in Colorado has shown that conventional tillage increases runoff of 
sediment-bound constituents relative to conservation tillage, while improvements in soil 
structure and residue retention are associated with reduced sediment export (Trimarco et al., 
2023). Similarly, studies in irrigated furrow systems demonstrate strong coupling between 

--- PAGE 5 ---
 
Page 5 of 73 
 
sediment transport and phosphorus losses in irrigation return flow (Bjorneberg, Westermann, et 
al., 2006; Kleinman et al., 2015). However, relatively few long-term (>5 years) edge-of-field 
(EoF) investigations have quantified tillage effects in surface-irrigated systems over extended 
time horizons (Sun et al., 2026). In addition, tillage effects are often evaluated as aggregate 
treatment differences, making it difficult to distinguish water-quality responses associated with 
soil disturbance from those associated with residue cover. This distinction is important because 
conservation tillage simultaneously reduces mechanical disturbance and increases surface 
residue, creating both direct and indirect pathways through which management may influence 
runoff, erosion, and constituent transport. Long-term analyses that include both tillage intensity 
and measured residue cover are therefore needed to distinguish transient responses from 
persistent system behavior and to better characterize the mechanisms underlying conservation-
tillage effects under variable operational conditions. 
Despite their importance, long-term EoF datasets in operational agricultural systems present 
substantial analytical challenges. Often arising from logistical and/or financial constraints, 
records commonly include irregular sampling, evolving instrumentation, missing observations, 
and measurement uncertainty, all of which complicate inference on management effects (Brown 
et al., 2026; Daniels et al., 2018; Harmel et al., 2006, 2023; Harmel, King, et al., 2018; King & 
Harmel, 1998). Process-based water quality models are widely applied to assess the effects of 
agricultural practices on water quality at many scales (Daniels et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2017; 
Marshall et al., 2018), but their application in EoF and long-term observational contexts is often 
constrained by high data requirements, parameter uncertainty, and challenges in calibration to 
site-specific conditions, particularly where management, soil, and weather interactions vary 
through time and space. These limitations are well documented in agricultural systems, where 

--- PAGE 6 ---
 
Page 6 of 73 
 
model performance is sensitive to parameterization and local conditions, and where equifinality 
and structural uncertainty can complicate inference from observational datasets (Badrudeen et 
al., 2026; Beven, 2006; Guzman et al., 2015; Harmel & King, 2005; Harmel & Smith, 2007; 
Moriasi et al., 2007; Yen et al., 2014). In contrast, empirical approaches that directly leverage 
observed data provide a complementary pathway for inference, particularly in contexts where the 
data required to parameterize and calibrate process-based models are incomplete, uncertain, or 
unavailable. By relying on observed relationships rather than fully specified system 
representations, empirical models reduce calibration burden, avoid issues of parameter non-
identifiability, and are more readily applied to long-term datasets characterized by variability in 
management, environmental conditions, and measurement systems. Recent work in on-farm 
experimentation and digital agronomy further highlights the need for analytical frameworks 
capable of extracting reliable insight from such data while accounting for spatial variability and 
limited replication (Cho et al., 2021; Córdoba et al., 2024). Moreover, causal inference 
frameworks offer a structured way to integrate mechanistic reasoning with observational data, 
enabling more conservative and interpretable conclusions by explicitly evaluating intermediary 
processes rather than relying solely on outcome-based associations (Longchamps, Lanza, Wei, et 
al., 2025; Longchamps, Lanza, Yore, et al., 2025). Together, these approaches support the 
development of statistical models that explicitly account for measurement error, process 
variability, and temporal dependence while preserving interpretable relationships among 
variables. 
Therefore, the present analysis aims to 1) quantify differences in runoff volume, concentration, 
and mass loadings among conventional, strip, and minimum tillage study treatments through 
time, 2) estimate analyte-specific direct effects of tillage intensity after accounting for measured 

--- PAGE 7 ---
 
Page 7 of 73 
 
residue cover and other measured covariates, and 3) evaluate the temporal persistence of tillage 
effects under long-term, operational field conditions. These objectives are addressed using a 
hierarchical Bayesian framework that jointly models runoff volume, concentration, and derived 
loads while accounting for measurement error, missing data, measured residue cover, and shared 
temporal structure across analytes. To assess persistence, the model incorporates a multi-output 
Gaussian process that captures temporal dependence and enables evaluation of whether observed 
treatment effects reflect transient responses or sustained system behavior. This integrated 
approach provides a framework for separating treatment-level water-quality patterns, direct 
tillage-intensity effects, and residue-associated pathways in complex, long-term EoF datasets. 
2 MATERIALS AND METHODS 
2.1 Study Site Description and Experimental History 
This study was conducted at the Colorado State University (CSU) Agricultural Research, 
Development, and Education Center (ARDEC) near Fort Collins, Colorado (40.68° N, 104.99° 
W; elevation approximately 1567 m), within a semi-arid, surface-irrigated production system 
representative of furrow-irrigated agriculture in the western United States. The 5.3-hectare field 
was selected by CSU researchers and an accompanying producer advisory board to evaluate how 
tillage practices influence runoff generation and water-quality transport under operational field 
conditions, where managed irrigation drives hydrologic processes distinct from rainfall-
dominated systems. 
The experiment was established in 2011 following a uniform conventional tillage baseline to 
compare three tillage systems: conventional tillage (CT), minimum tillage (MT), and strip tillage 
(ST). Treatments were implemented in a randomized complete block design with two blocks and 

--- PAGE 8 ---
 
Page 8 of 73 
 
each treatment replicated once per block. Individual plots were approximately 320 m long and 28 
m wide (i.e., 0.9 ha), reflecting production-scale field dimensions necessary to capture realistic 
furrow flow dynamics and EoF runoff processes (Figure 1).

Soils at the site are classified as Garrett fine-loamy, mixed, mesic Pachic Argiustolls based on 
the USDA NRCS Soil Survey (Soil Survey Staff, 2003). The soil profile is characterized by 
approximately 52% sand, 18% silt, and 30% clay, with an average organic matter content of 
1.8% and a pH of 7.8. The region receives approximately 407 mm of annual precipitation, with 
mean monthly temperatures ranging from 2.7 °C in January to 17.6 °C in July, reinforcing the 
reliance on irrigation for crop production. Irrigation was applied using a siphon tube system from 
2011 – 2022 and a gated pipe system thereafter, supplied by a groundwater well in most years 
but surface water in instances where the well was inoperative or insufficient, with water 
delivered to furrows during controlled irrigation events typically occurring between 06:00 and 
18:00 hours (i.e., 10 – 12 hour sets). Flow was directed down alternating rows, generating 
tailwater runoff that was collected and measured at the downstream edge of each plot. Other 
management practices, including crop rotation, residue handling, and irrigation scheduling, were 
adapted annually in response to environmental conditions and operational constraints, reflecting 
realistic production-scale management rather than a fixed experimental protocol. 
2.2 Tillage Management and Field Operations 
Tillage management at the study site was implemented through a sequence of field operations 
that varied in type, intensity, and timing across treatments and years. CT generally involved 
multiple full-width disturbance operations, including moldboard plowing, disking, and 
harrowing, followed by furrow maintenance and planting operations. MT reduced both the 
number and intensity of disturbance passes, typically relying on vertical tillage and limited soil 
manipulation to maintain irrigation function. ST further minimized disturbance by restricting soil 
disruption to narrow bands along the seed row using a strip-till implement, while maintaining 

--- PAGE 10 ---
 
Page 10 of 73 
 
residue cover between rows. All treatments included some level of row maintenance and 
cultivation to preserve furrow irrigation performance (e.g., row cleaner with cans). 
Fertilizer management also differed among treatments in ways that influenced soil disturbance 
and nutrient placement. Conventional and minimum tillage systems typically received dry 
broadcast fertilizer applications prior to incorporation, whereas strip-till systems consistently 
utilized liquid nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) applied in bands during the strip-till or planting 
operation. When applicable, such as during grain and silage corn production years, nitrogen side-
dressing was applied across all treatments to meet crop nutrient demands. 
Field operations, including tillage, crop type, planting, cultivation, fertilizer and pesticide 
application, and harvest, were recorded by CSU ARDEC staff using FarmLogic farm 
management software (TapLogic LLC, Murray, KY, USA). These records included operation 
date, implement type, and treatment designation.  
Despite consistent treatment designations, the specific combination and intensity of operations 
varied substantially from year to year in response to crop rotation, weather conditions, irrigation 
requirements, and operational constraints. For example, during a representative grain corn year 
(2015), CT included multiple full-width disturbance operations, while MT and ST reduced both 
the number and intensity of passes, with ST concentrating disturbance within the seedbed (Figure 
2). In contrast, certain years exhibited minimal differentiation among treatments. In 2023, corn 
was harvested as silage and all plots were uniformly vertical-tilled immediately prior to planting 
winter wheat in order to meet narrow agronomic timing requirements ahead of forecasted 
precipitation. These examples illustrate that categorical treatment labels do not fully capture the 
dynamic nature of soil disturbance in production systems.

2.3 Quantification of tillage intensity using STIR 
Because implemented tillage operations varied across years and occasionally converged among 
treatments for agronomic or operational reasons, disturbance intensity was quantified using the 
Soil Tillage Intensity Rating (STIR) rather than relying solely on categorical CT, MT, and ST 
designations. STIR provides a continuous, operation-based measure of mechanical soil 
disturbance that standardizes tillage intensity across years and management adjustments (Foster 
et al., 2003; Renard et al., 2017). STIR integrates implement speed, tillage depth, a tillage type 
modifier (TTM), and the proportion of soil surface disturbed into a dimensionless index 
calibrated to erosion-relevant energy units. Values for each field operation were derived using 
the Management Operations and Soil Erosion Simulation (MOSES) database distributed by 
USDA–NRCS and implemented via the SoilManageR R package (Heller et al., 2025; Meyer et 
al., 2001). The STIR value for a given operation was calculated as:

2.4 Residue characterization 
Crop residue cover was measured to characterize soil-surface protection associated with each 
tillage system and to provide residue context for the water-quality analysis. Residue cover was 
estimated using a line-transect method adapted from UNL and NRCS residue measurement 
procedures (Shelton et al., 1990; USDA-NRCS, 2011). Measurements were collected annually 
within a week of crop planting for each of the six plots when field conditions and project 
logistics allowed. 
Within each plot, three transect locations were selected to represent the north, middle, and south 
portions of the plot (i.e., a sub-plot grid). In each sub-plot, a point was selected randomly and 
used as the center of an X-shaped transect layout. Each arm of the X was 30.5 m long (100 ft) 
and was oriented diagonally across the crop rows. Transects crossed six 0.762 m beds, equivalent 
to six 30-inch beds. Surface cover was recorded at 0.305 m intervals (1 ft) along each transect 
arm.

residue, bare soil, living vegetation, human trash, or other surface cover. Only crop residue was 
counted toward percent residue cover (Figure 3).

2.5 Flow monitoring and water quality sampling 
Six EoF monitoring sites were installed in the study field, approximately 8 m from the lower 
field edge, with one site corresponding to each tillage research plot. Each plot was 36 seed beds 
wide, with 0.762 m spacing between beds (i.e., 30-inch), and the EoF monitoring site was placed 
near the plot center on rows 17 through 19. The two irrigated rows contributing to each 
monitoring site, rows 17 and 19, were diverted into row 17 to provide sufficient flow for sample 
collection and to reduce ponding around installed flumes. Because the study was maintained as a 
long-term field experiment under operational agricultural conditions, monitoring infrastructure, 
stage measurement technology, and sampling protocols evolved over the 14-year monitoring 
period. 
2.5.1 Flow monitoring infrastructure 
EoF monitoring sites consisted of flow-control structures positioned to capture plot-level outflow 
from furrow-irrigation events. Flow-measurement infrastructure varied through time as 
equipment was replaced, refined, or upgraded. From 2011 through 2021, galvanized metal V-
notch flumes, including 7-inch and 10-inch configurations, were deployed at each EoF site. From 
2022 through 2025, runoff was monitored using fiberglass 60-degree trapezoidal flumes 
(Robinson & Chamberlain, 1960). 
Runoff stage and discharge were measured using a combination of automated and manual field 
methods depending on study year, equipment availability, and sensor reliability. Pressure 
transducers placed in flume stilling wells were used during early study years, including YSI 

--- PAGE 15 ---
 
Page 15 of 73 
 
Level Scout pressure transducers from 2011 through 2013 (YSI Incorporated, Yellow Springs, 
OH). Teledyne ISCO 730 bubbler units (Teledyne ISCO, Lincoln, NE) were used as the primary 
automated stage measurement system from 2014 through 2025. Additional sensor technologies 
were evaluated or deployed during later monitoring years, including Mileone eTape sensors 
(Mileone Technologies, Inc., Sewell, NJ) from 2021 through 2025 and HOBO U20L-01 Water 
Level Loggers (HOBO Data Loggers, Bourne, MA) in 2023. Manual ruler measurements were 
collected throughout the study period to measure water depth when automated measurements 
were unavailable and to calibrate, verify, or correct automated stage measurements when 
possible. 
Stage observations were converted to runoff discharge and event volume using the relevant 
rating relationship for each flow-control structure. Because flow measurement technology and 
reliability varied across the monitoring record, each observation was assigned metadata 
describing the flume type and measurement method used. Years or events associated with 
experimental sensors, incomplete deployment, equipment failure, or otherwise unreliable stage 
records were flagged during quality assurance. When runoff volume could not be estimated 
defensibly (e.g., equipment failure), the associated volume record was treated as unobserved 
(i.e., missing) in the final dataset rather than assigned an assumed or calculated value. Events 
where irrigation occurred but no plot outflow was observed, such as under extremely dry soil 
conditions, were recorded as zero volume.  
Inflow volume was measured for each plot during each irrigation event. For each plot and 
irrigation set, inflow rate was estimated using two replicate timed fills of a 7 L bucket from each 
of the two irrigation inflow rows. Replicate fill times were averaged and converted to an inflow 

--- PAGE 16 ---
 
Page 16 of 73 
 
rate, which was then multiplied by the recorded irrigation duration for that plot to estimate total 
inflow volume.  
2.5.2 Water quality sampling approaches 
Water-quality samples were collected at each plot’s outflow using multiple sampling approaches 
over the long-term study period (Figure 4). Inflow water-quality samples were also collected 
during irrigation events, whenever possible, from the concrete ditch, siphon tube, or gated-pipe 
outlet used to deliver irrigation water. Inflow samples generally consisted of a primary sample 
and field duplicate when feasible and were used to characterize source-water chemistry 
associated with each irrigation event.

Sampling methods at EoF sites varied over the long-term record as project objectives, labor 
availability, monitoring technology, and equipment changed. Each sample was assigned a 
method code describing how the sample was collected. Grab samples (GB) consisted of a 
manually collected point sample at a single time without a technical sampler and were not 
composited. Grab samples from a temporary V-notch weir (GBW), used during early monitoring 
years, were collected manually with a bottle from water passing through an elevated V-notch 
weir installed in the furrow. Hourly grab composites (GBH) consisted of manually collected grab 
samples composited across an irrigation event or sampling window at approximately hourly 
intervals. Three-part grab composites (GB3), used in 2023, combined subsamples representing 
first-flush, first-hour, and last-hour runoff conditions. Automated samples were collected using 
either commercial ISCO 6712 samplers (ISCO; Teledyne ISCO, Lincoln, NE) or low-cost 
automated samplers (LC) developed by the CSU Agricultural Water Quality Program (Deleon et 
al., 2025). ISCO and LC samplers collected water automatically from the outflow monitoring 
location according to the sampling program used for that event. 
These sample types were retained as explicit metadata because they represent different levels of 
temporal integration within runoff events. GB and GBW samples represented point-in-time 
conditions, GBH and GB3 samples represented manually composited event or within-event 
conditions, and ISCO and LC samples represented automated sampler collections. This 
classification allowed the long-term dataset to preserve information on whether each 
concentration value reflected a single grab, a manual composite, or an automated sample, rather 
than treating all water-quality observations as equivalent measurements. 
For each water-quality observation, detailed sample metadata were retained to identify the 
method by which the sample was collected and how it should be interpreted analytically.

Page 19 of 73 
 
Metadata fields included sampling method, composite type, duplicate status, first-flush 
designation, measurement method, laboratory identifier, and other quality-control notes. 
Duplicate samples, when available, were retained to characterize field variability. This structure 
allowed the final dataset to distinguish among observed concentrations, missing samples, such as 
samples lost through spills or sampler malfunction, and samples collected under conditions 
requiring special interpretation. 
In 2023 and 2024, this study site also served as a dedicated sampler comparison experiment 
evaluating several EoF water-sampling approaches under the same field conditions as the long-
term dataset. That work found broad agreement among sampling methods for most analytes, 
supporting the use of multiple sampling approaches in long-term EoF monitoring when 
collection methods and site configuration are documented. However, the study also showed that 
apparent sampler differences can arise from interactions among flume morphology, intake 
placement, and automated sampler purge procedures rather than from sampler type alone (Brown 
et al., 2026). The present 14-year analysis extends that framework to a longer historical record 
with additional sampling methods, but also including flow measurement type, and measurement 
metadata needed to account for method-related uncertainty.

and trace elements using methods generally consistent with NRCS CEMA 201 guidance for 
edge-of-field monitoring where possible (USDA-NRCS, 2012). Because this study spans 14 
years, analytical laboratories, method availability, detection limits, reporting limits, and project 
objectives changed over time. Therefore, the methods listed below represent the primary 
analytical approaches used for the final analytes included in this analysis, while year-specific

2.7 Data preparation 
Data processing was conducted using a scripted workflow that integrated water-quality records, 
runoff-volume estimates, residue-cover measurements, tillage operation records, and crop 
records into a common long-format analytical dataset. The purpose of this workflow was to 
preserve the long-term monitoring record while maintaining traceability of laboratory methods, 
field measurement methods, crop-season assignments, treatment history, and missing or censored 
observations. 
2.7.1 Data processing and quality control 
Water-quality records were screened for laboratory qualifiers, non-detect flags, duplicate status, 
sampling method, laboratory identifier, analytical method, and reporting or detection-limit 
information. Values reported below detection or reporting limits were retained with censoring 
information when available. Qualifier flags, estimated-value flags, holding-time notes, laboratory 
comments, and other quality-control metadata were preserved in the analytical dataset when 

--- PAGE 22 ---
 
Page 22 of 73 
 
available. Duplicate samples were retained and explicitly identified so that duplicate status could 
be represented in the statistical model. 
Runoff-volume records were screened separately from concentration records. Events with 
observed irrigation but no plot runoff were coded using a no-runoff indicator and distinguished 
from events where runoff volume was unavailable because of equipment failure, incomplete 
stage records, experimental sensor deployment, or other field limitations. Missing runoff-volume 
values were retained as missing values rather than replaced by assumed values. For the final 
Bayesian model, runoff volume was indexed at the event-volume-unit level and then mapped to 
analyte-specific concentration rows, preventing repeated analyte rows from being treated as 
independent volume measurements. 
Residue-cover records were screened for plot, treatment, replicate, year, sampling position, and 
percent-cover values. Transect-level measurements from the north, middle, and south portions of 
each plot were averaged to obtain plot-level residue cover for each measurement year when 
available. Missing transect or plot-year residue measurements were retained as missing values. 
For Bayesian model inputs, residue cover was converted from percent cover to a proportion. 
Values of 0% and 100% were adjusted slightly inward so that all residue-cover values fell within 
the open interval required for logit-scale modeling. The residue-cover dataset used in this 
analysis is provided as Supplementary Material S1. 
Tillage records were screened for date consistency, treatment assignment, duplicate entries, 
missing operation names, and user-entry differences where different labels referred to the same 
implement or field activity. Operation names were standardized to a common tillage-operation 
key, and implement assignments were checked against field notes and supplemental records 

--- PAGE 23 ---
 
Page 23 of 73 
 
when available. The cleaned tillage records were normalized into a master operation table 
containing date, treatment system, operation type, and STIR input information for each field 
activity. STIR was calculated for each recorded operation and accumulated within crop-season 
windows. Seasonal STIR was reset immediately after harvest, such that tillage operations 
occurring after harvest were assigned to the following crop season. For each water-quality 
sampling date, season-to-date STIR represented cumulative disturbance from the previous crop 
harvest through the sampling date. The tillage operation records used to calculate STIR are 
provided as Supplementary Material S2. 
Crop records were screened for crop type, planting date, harvest date, and yield information 
when available. These records were used to define crop-season windows, assign water-quality 
observations to crop seasons, and identify the previous crop associated with each observation for 
the residue submodel. The crop records used for seasonal assignment and previous-crop 
classification are provided as Supplementary Material S3. 
Event chronology was preserved using date, year, irrigation number, treatment, replicate, and 
inflow or outflow designation. Records with partial information were retained when the available 
fields could be linked reliably to an irrigation event, plot, treatment, and analyte. Field notes 
were retained as a notes field and used to document year-specific issues, including 
instrumentation transitions, sampling limitations, laboratory changes, irrigation infrastructure 
changes, funding-related gaps, and unusual field conditions. 
2.7.2 Dataset integration 
The dataset assembly workflow consisted of three primary integration steps. First, water-quality 
data were converted from wide-format laboratory records to a long-format structure in which 

--- PAGE 24 ---
 
Page 24 of 73 
 
each row represented a single sample, analyte, and result. The long-format table retained sample

Second, management records were prepared for integration with the water-quality dataset.

Third, water-quality observations were assigned to crop-season windows based on sampling date

with one row per analyte-specific outflow observation when available. Key derived fields 
included crop-season metadata, plot-level residue cover, season-to-date STIR from the previous 
crop harvest through the sample date, inflow chemistry, inflow volume, outflow concentration, 
outflow volume, censoring information, and field-method metadata. 
Data processing and dataset assembly were implemented in Python using pandas for tabular data 
manipulation and NumPy for numerical operations (Harris et al., 2020; McKinney, 2010; Python 
Software Foundation, 2023). The final model-ready dataset spanned the 2011 through 2025

monitoring period and retained years with partial records, instrumentation transitions, laboratory

2.8 Bayesian modeling framework for tillage effects on runoff and water quality 
2.8.1 Directed acyclic graph and target estimand 
A directed acyclic graph (DAG; Figure 5) was used to define the assumed data-generating 
process before fitting the statistical model. The DAG was used for two purposes. First, it 
clarified the target estimand as the direct effect of season-to-date STIR on latent runoff 
concentration and latent runoff volume, conditional on the measured and modeled mediating and 
adjustment variables included in the model. Second, it identified the variables needed to separate 
this direct effect from confounding, measurement heterogeneity, censoring, missing 
observations, and shared temporal structure. This approach follows the general causal workflow 
in which a graphical model is used to state assumptions, define the estimand, and then translate 
those assumptions into a statistical model rather than treating regression adjustment as a purely 
algorithmic variable-selection exercise (Gelman et al., 2026; McElreath, 2020; Pearl, 2009, 
2012) .

2.8.3 Priors and computation 
Prior distributions were selected to regularize estimation on standardized scales while allowing 
effects large enough to be detected when supported by the data. Regression slopes in the 
concentration, volume, and residue submodels were assigned zero-centered weakly informative 
normal priors, commonly 𝑁𝑜𝑟𝑚𝑎𝑙(0, 1), reflecting the expectation that a one standard deviation 
change in a predictor would usually produce a one standard deviation change in the response 
while still allowing larger effects when supported by the likelihood. 
A key feature of the model was the use of non-centered multivariate normal prior structures for 
related parameter groups. The analyte-specific concentration intercepts, direct STIR slopes, 
inflow-concentration slopes, and duplicate-sample effects were modeled jointly so that 
information could be partially pooled across analytes while allowing correlations among these 
related coefficients. Block, sampler-method, and flume-method effects were also parameterized

using non-centered multivariate normal structures, allowing the model to estimate structured 
variation associated with experimental design and measurement heterogeneity rather than 
treating each level as an unrelated fixed adjustment. In addition, irrigation-count slopes were 
modeled as correlated analyte-specific effects.

Focusing on the impacts of tillage disturbance intensity (i.e., STIR) on the system over time,

3.1 Final analytical dataset and realized tillage disturbance 
The final analytical dataset combined long-term water-quality observations with crop history, 
residue cover, runoff-volume estimates, inflow conditions, sampling metadata, and calculated 
STIR values for the 2011 to 2025 monitoring period. This integrated structure allowed each 
analyte-specific outflow observation to be linked to the crop season, previous crop, plot-level 
residue cover, event-level runoff volume, inflow concentration, inflow volume, field 
measurement method, laboratory method, and cumulative soil disturbance history associated 
with that sampling event. 
Figure 6 shows season-to-date STIR accumulation by tillage system from 2011 through 2025, 
with values resetting after each crop harvest. These seasonal trajectories show how mechanical 
disturbance accumulated within individual crop seasons and how strongly CT separated from the 
two conservation tillage systems. Across the full monitoring period, total cumulative STIR was 
greatest under CT (2,894), whereas MT and ST accumulated substantially lower total disturbance 
(1,158 and 1,156, respectively). Although MT and ST were implemented using distinct 
conservation tillage practices, their cumulative STIR totals were nearly identical, indicating that 
both systems produced similar overall mechanical disturbance relative to CT.

3.2 Direct effects of STIR on water quality 
The analyte-specific concentration STIR effects are shown in Figure 7. Posterior distributions for 
TSS, TKN, and TP are shifted clearly to the right of zero, indicating that higher STIR was 
associated with higher concentrations of these analytes. Se and OP also show positive median 
effects, although with broader uncertainty. By contrast, the distributions for NH4-N, TDS, NO3-
N, and NO2-N remain closer to zero and overlap it more strongly, indicating weaker or less 
certain relationships with soil disturbance. This contrast between particulate-associated and more 
dissolved constituents is consistent with the broader tillage and water-quality literature, which 
shows that reduced disturbance and greater residue cover are effective at reducing sediment and 

--- PAGE 37 ---
 
Page 37 of 73 
 
sediment-bound nutrient transport, while dissolved constituents are often more weakly or 
inconsistently tied to surface soil disturbance alone (Baulch et al., 2019; Duncan et al., 2019; 
Prasad, Thomason, et al., 2023). In irrigated furrow systems, sediment transport is also closely 
coupled to phosphorus movement, making positive STIR effects on particulate analytes 
particularly plausible in this setting (Bjorneberg, Aase, et al., 2006).

These concentration responses translate into comparable event-scale loading patterns when

The darker ribbons represent uncertainty in the estimated mean relationship between STIR and

which describes the range of individual observations expected around that mean relationship. 
Predictive uncertainty, or the inherent imprecision and range of possible outcomes in forecasts 
by the generative model, includes multiple sources of variability: natural process variability (e.g., 
year effects, block effects, temporal MOGP deviations, and residual variability representing 
unmodeled physical variability), as well as observation uncertainty (e.g., measurement error in 
the derivation of concentration values and runoff volume). As a result, the predictive intervals for 
each analyte are substantially wider than that of the mean STIR-load response. Because event 
loads are computed from the modeled latent “true” concentration and runoff-volume states rather 
than directly from noisy observations, the model explicitly separates measurement error from 
underlying process relationships. This hierarchical structure allows uncertainty in the observation 
process to be accounted for in estimating the mean STIR-load response from the latent 
environmental states, providing clearer inference about management effects than approaches that 
rely solely on observed measurements (e.g., machine-learning). 
The resulting mean relationships consistently indicate that increasing STIR tends to elevate mass 
export for analytes most closely associated with sediment mobilization. The persistence of 
positive STIR-load relationships despite wide predictive intervals indicates that the management 
signal is present but embedded within substantial real-world hydrologic variability, which is

above zero). Tillage can alter soil structure, infiltration, surface roughness, and furrow flow 30 
hydraulics in ways that do not produce a universal hydrologic response across sites or 31 
management systems (Berehe et al., 2013; Bjorneberg, Aase, et al., 2006; Munkholm et al., 32 
2013). In surface-irrigated fields specifically, runoff is controlled not only by soil properties but 33 
also by managed inflow, advance time, infiltration opportunity time, and tailwater hydraulics, 34 
which can complicate simple expectations derived from rainfall-driven systems (Berehe et al., 35 
2013; Bjorneberg, Aase, et al., 2006). One plausible explanation for the modest negative direct 36 
STIR effect is the role of freshly disturbed furrow roughness in surface irrigation. A newly tilled 37 
field may have slower advance times than the same field after rows have been pre-irrigated or 38 
partially smoothed by prior flow. Greater surface roughness would reduce flow velocity, thereby 39 
increasing infiltration opportunity time and reducing the fraction of applied water that exits the 40 
field as tailwater runoff, provided infiltration capacity is not simultaneously reduced enough to 41 
offset that hydraulic effect. Evidence from furrow-irrigation studies supports this interpretation. 42 
Berehe et al. (2013) found that deeper tillage increased advance time and irrigation infiltration in 43 
a furrow-irrigated system, and Bryant et al. (2021) showed that conservation tillage systems can 44 
alter furrow-irrigation efficiency through changes in infiltration and flow behavior. Under this 45 
interpretation, a recently cultivated corn field, such as after sidedressing or cultivation, could 46 
temporarily exhibit less runoff because roughness and infiltration opportunity act together to 47 
slow water movement through the furrow. Thus, the negative direct STIR effect is physically 48 
plausible, even if it is weaker than the residue-mediated pathway. 49

3.4 Temporal persistence of tillage 63 
The long-term record also indicates that some water-quality responses are not purely 64 
contemporaneous. The year-distance covariance functions from the multi-output Gaussian 65 
process (MOGP) show how residual year effects remain correlated as the temporal distance 66 
between years increases, after accounting for STIR, residue cover, irrigation count, inflow 67 
conditions, crop effects, sampling method, flume method, and other predictors included in the 68 
hierarchical model (Error! Reference source not found.). These temporal effects represent 69 
residual year-to-year variation in analyte concentrations that remains after conditioning on other 70 
modeled covariates. Larger covariance at greater year distances therefore indicates stronger 71 
temporal persistence, whereas rapid decay toward zero indicates that unexplained deviations are 72 
mostly limited to the current year. Because these latent temporal effects persist after accounting 73 
for the modeled predictors, they likely reflect broader system-level influences not included in the 74 
dataset, such as soil characteristics that may impact water quality such as bulk density and 75 
electrical conductivity (Trimarco et al., 2025). 76

The posterior covariance functions suggest that temporal persistence was analyte-specific. Total 86 
nitrogen (TN-N) and total phosphorus (TP) showed clearer residual covariance across adjacent 87 
years than several other analytes, consistent with the interpretation that N and P responses may 88 
partly reflect legacy nutrient pools, soil structural change, residue-mediated nutrient cycling, or 89 
hydrologic transport pathways that do not reset completely between growing seasons. This 90 
interpretation is consistent with broader evidence that soil structure, residue dynamics, and 91 
erosion susceptibility can carry management legacies across years rather than resetting 92 
completely after each crop cycle (Grahmann et al., 2022; Larney et al., 2016; Munkholm et al., 93 
2013). It is also consistent with the broader water-quality literature showing that legacy 94 
phosphorus accumulated from past management can continue to influence future P losses, and 95 
that nitrogen legacies and hydrologic lag times can delay water-quality responses after changes 96 
in management (Golden et al., 2023; Sharpley et al., 2013). 97 
The strong posterior covariance for NO2-N should be interpreted cautiously. Nitrite is a transient 98 
intermediate in the nitrogen cycle, and the concentration-effect estimates indicated that NO2-N 99 
was not strongly explained by tillage intensity, with comparatively wide uncertainty (Figure 7). 100 
The MOGP covariance function may therefore be capturing temporally structured residual 101 
variation that was not explained by the measured covariates rather than indicating a direct nitrite 102 
legacy effect. In this sense, the NO2-N pattern is useful as a diagnostic result: it suggests that 103 
unmeasured processes, such as short-term redox conditions, nitrification and denitrification 104 
timing, fertilizer timing relative to irrigation, soil moisture dynamics, microbial activity, or other 105 
year-specific biogeochemical controls, may be important for explaining NO2-N behavior in this 106 
system. This interpretation is consistent with the purpose of the temporal covariance term, which 107 
is to characterize residual year-to-year structure after accounting for observed predictors, not to 108

identify the specific mechanism responsible for that structure. Additional targeted measurements 109 
or complementary modeling approaches, including more mechanistic N-cycling models or 110 
flexible predictive models (e.g., machine learning), should be used to evaluate why NO2-N 111

and TN, which were selected for the main text because they represent sediment and nutrient 116

physical water-quality impairment, while TP and TN are central nutrient indicators associated 118 
with downstream eutrophication risk. Together, these analytes provide a management-facing 119 
summary of how reduced disturbance affected both sediment and nutrient export over the long-120 
term monitoring period. 121

For implementation, these results suggest that conservation tillage should be understood as both 139 
an immediate and cumulative water-quality practice. Immediate benefits arise because reduced 140 
disturbance lowers sediment detachment potential and preserves residue cover within the current 141 
season. Longer term benefits arise because lower annual exports occur repeatedly through time 142 
and because some water-quality responses show temporal persistence beyond the year of 143 
disturbance. Thus, Figure 13 does not imply that reduced tillage will produce large treatment 144 
separation every year. Rather, it shows that reduced disturbance can shift the long-term 145 
distribution of annual loads downward, producing meaningful cumulative reductions even when 146 
individual years are dominated by hydrologic variability. 147

These cumulative patterns are consistent with the concentration, event-load, residue, and 168 
temporal-persistence results presented earlier. Constituents most closely associated with 169 
sediment and particulate transport showed the clearest response to reduced disturbance, whereas 170 
more dissolved constituents exhibited weaker or less consistent responses. This pattern supports 171 
the interpretation that the primary water-quality benefit of reduced tillage in this system was 172 
erosion control and reduced mobilization of sediment-associated nutrients, especially TSS, TP, 173 
and organic nitrogen fractions. These findings are consistent with prior syntheses showing that 174 
conservation tillage and residue retention are most effective for reducing runoff pollutants linked 175 
to sediment detachment and transport, while dissolved nutrient responses may depend more 176 
strongly on nutrient placement, timing, hydrology, and source-water chemistry (Prasad, 177 
Thomason, et al., 2023). 178 
From a producer-facing standpoint, these results suggest that adopting some form of 179 
conservation tillage was more important than the specific reduced-tillage label applied. Although 180 
MT and ST were implemented using distinct operations, their cumulative STIR totals were 181 
nearly identical and their cumulative water-quality outcomes were similar. Producers may 182 
therefore have flexibility to choose the reduced-disturbance system that best fits their equipment, 183 
crop rotation, irrigation constraints, residue-management goals, and economic conditions, while 184 

--- PAGE 54 ---
 
Page 54 of 73 
still achieving meaningful reductions in sediment and sediment-associated nutrient export 185 
relative to CT. The central management implication is therefore not that one conservation tillage 186 
system was universally superior, but that reducing mechanical disturbance and maintaining 187 
surface cover provided measurable long-term water-quality benefits compared with continued 188 
full-width conventional tillage. 189 
The temporal-persistence results also indicate that tillage decisions should not be evaluated only 190 
in the year they occur. Some benefits of conservation tillage are expected immediately through 191 
reduced disturbance and greater surface cover, whereas additional benefits likely accrue over 192 
multiple seasons as residue, soil structure, nutrient pools, and hydrologic pathways adjust. 193 
Conversely, occasional high-disturbance operations may have carryover consequences beyond a 194 
single season. Conservation programs and producer evaluations should therefore consider both 195 
annual outcomes and multi-year cumulative responses when assessing the water-quality value of 196 
reduced tillage. 197 
The weaker responses for dissolved constituents also indicate that reduced tillage alone should 198 
not be expected to control all water-quality pathways. Management strategies targeting soluble 199 
nutrient transport will likely require complementary practices, including nutrient source, rate, 200 
timing, and placement management, irrigation scheduling, and runoff-volume reduction. In this 201 
sense, conservation tillage appears most effective as part of a broader conservation system: it can 202 
reduce sediment and sediment-associated nutrient export, while complementary nutrient and 203 
irrigation practices are needed to address dissolved nutrient and salt transport. 204

3.6 Limitations 205 
This analysis was designed primarily for inference about tillage effects within a long-term EoF 206 
experiment, not as a transferable prediction tool for other sites. The Bayesian model was 207 
intentionally structured to estimate direct STIR effects, residue-associated pathways, event-level 208 
runoff volume, temporal persistence, and cumulative mass loads while accounting for 209 
measurement heterogeneity, censoring, and missing data. As a result, the model required detailed 210 
covariate information that is uncommon in many monitoring datasets, including sampling 211 
method, duplicate status, flume type, stage or flow measurement method, inflow concentration, 212 
inflow volume, residue cover, crop history, and operation-level STIR values. Although the 213 
general DAG and modeling framework could be adapted to other EoF datasets, the fitted model 214 
should not be interpreted as directly transportable to sites lacking comparable covariate detail or 215 
similar irrigation, soil, and management conditions. 216 
The study was conducted at a single surface-irrigated site in northern Colorado. The long 217 
monitoring period strengthens inference about temporal behavior within this field system, but the 218 
spatial scope remains limited. Responses to tillage may differ in rainfall-driven systems, 219 
subsurface-drained fields, different soil textures, different irrigation technologies, or systems 220 
with different nutrient-management histories. Therefore, the estimated effects should be 221 
interpreted as broad evidence for a furrow-irrigated, semi-arid production system rather than as 222 
universal effect sizes for conservation tillage. Multi-site studies using similar causal and 223 
uncertainty-aware frameworks would be needed to determine how general these relationships are 224 
across irrigation systems, climates, soils, and management histories. 225 
The causal interpretation depends on the DAG and the measured adjustment set. The model 226 
estimates direct STIR effects conditional on residue and other modeled covariates, however, 227 

--- PAGE 56 ---
 
Page 56 of 73 
some potential mechanisms were not measured consistently across the full record, including 228 
infiltration, aggregate stability, bulk density, soil moisture, furrow roughness, soil electrical 229 
conductivity, nutrient stratification, and other soil physical or chemical properties. The year-by-230 
analyte Gaussian process captures shared temporal structure remaining after modeled covariates 231 
are considered, but those latent year effects should not be interpreted as identifying a specific 232 
physical mechanism. 233 
Long-term monitoring heterogeneity also creates unavoidable uncertainty. Laboratory methods, 234 
analytical suites, flow-measurement technologies, sampling approaches, and project objectives 235 
changed during the 2011 to 2025 record. The Bayesian framework was developed specifically to 236 
retain these data rather than discard incomplete years or rely on substitution methods for 237 
censored observations. The present analysis provides probabilistic evidence about plausible 238 
mechanisms, direct effects, persistence, and long-term treatment differences, but it should not be 239 
treated as a mechanistic process model of erosion, infiltration, or nutrient transport. 240 
Future work should build on this inference-focused framework in three directions. First, 241 
additional long-term EoF studies should collect covariates that more directly represent soil 242 
physical mechanisms, including residue architecture, infiltration, aggregate stability, bulk 243 
density, soil moisture, furrow roughness, nutrient stratification, and soil nutrient status. These 244 
measurements would help separate the latent temporal structure currently absorbed by year 245 
effects into more interpretable physical pathways. Second, the posterior relationships estimated 246 
here should be compared with process-based models such as SWAT+, RZWQM2, or other 247 
mechanistic water-quality models capable of representing tillage, residue, soil-water movement, 248 
erosion, and nutrient cycling. Such comparisons would help determine whether the empirical 249 
causal patterns identified here are consistent with mechanistic expectations, where process-based 250 

--- PAGE 57 ---
 
Page 57 of 73 
models reproduce or fail to reproduce observed treatment differences, and which missing 251 
processes may be needed to improve representation of conservation tillage effects in irrigated 252 
systems. Third, prediction-focused tools should be developed separately from the causal model 253 
used here. Physically informed machine-learning models, Bayesian machine-learning 254 
approaches, or hybrid models that combine approaches could provide more pragmatic 255 
forecasting tools for annual load estimation. Such models would ideally rely on covariates that 256 
are routinely available to monitoring programs while still respecting known hydrologic and 257 
biogeochemical constraints. In this study, the primary objective was not to build a general 258 
prediction machine, but to clarify how tillage disturbance, residue cover, runoff volume, and 259 
temporal persistence shaped water-quality outcomes within a complex long-term monitoring 260 
record. 261 
4 CONCLUSION 262 
This study shows that realized tillage intensity is a useful lens for interpreting long-term edge-of-263 
field water-quality outcomes in a surface-irrigated production system. By replacing nominal 264 
treatment labels with season-to-date STIR, the analysis captured the operational reality that 265 
conservation systems vary through time and that water-quality responses are tied to the amount 266 
of mechanical disturbance actually imposed on the field. Across the 2011 to 2025 record, 267 
reduced-disturbance systems consistently lowered cumulative sediment and sediment-associated 268 
nutrient exports relative to conventional tillage, with the clearest benefits for TSS, TP, and 269 
organic nitrogen fractions. 270 
The practical implication is that conservation tillage should be evaluated as a multi-year 271 
management strategy rather than as a single-season intervention. Reduced disturbance can 272 
provide immediate benefits by limiting sediment detachment and preserving surface residue, but 273 

--- PAGE 58 ---
 
Page 58 of 73 
the broader water-quality value also accrues through repeated annual reductions and legacy 274 
effects that persist beyond the year of a given field operation. The similarity between MT and ST 275 
outcomes further suggests that producers may have flexibility in how they reduce disturbance; 276 
the specific conservation-tillage label may matter less than maintaining enough residue and 277 
reducing enough mechanical disturbance to limit runoff-driven sediment and nutrient transport. 278 
More broadly, this work demonstrates how uncertainty-aware Bayesian causal modeling can turn 279 
an imperfect long-term monitoring record into defensible inference about management effects. 280 
The framework retained censored observations, incomplete years, changing measurement 281 
methods, missing covariates, and shared temporal structure rather than treating those 282 
complications as reasons to discard data. For conservation research and practice, this is the larger 283 
contribution: long-term field datasets do not need to be clean or perfectly balanced to be 284 
scientifically useful, but they do require models that respect how the data were generated. In this 285 
system, that approach revealed that conservation tillage provides measurable and persistent 286 
water-quality benefits, while also showing that dissolved nutrient pathways will likely require 287 
complementary nutrient and irrigation management practices. 288
